## About This Recipe

Ezekiel 4:9 describes a bread made from a mixture of grains and legumes:

> "Take thou also unto thee wheat, and barley, and beans, and lentiles, and
> millet, and fitches, and put them in one vessel, and make thee bread
> thereof." — Ezekiel 4:9 (KJV)

This recipe is a historically informed reconstruction. It preserves the
multi-grain concept from Ezekiel 4:9 while acknowledging that the passage
provides no proportions, quantities, or cooking instructions.

## Historical Context

The passage places this bread in a prophetic and siege context. God
instructs Ezekiel to prepare it as a sign-act representing the bread the
Israelites would eat during the siege of Jerusalem. Ezekiel 4:10-15
describes eating it by weight and with impure water, reinforcing the
emergency context.

This is not a daily bread recipe. It is a deliberate, symbolically loaded
emergency food — bread made from whatever grains and legumes would be
available during a siege, mixed together in desperation.

### CRITICAL: Modern Commercial "Ezekiel Bread"

The modern commercial "Ezekiel bread" product (popularized by Food for Life)
draws on this passage but is a modern interpretation, not a historical
reconstruction. Any biblical-era version would have been vastly different
from the modern product — likely a coarse, dense flatbread rather than a
sliced loaf.

This recipe does NOT represent the commercial product as historical evidence.

## Translation Notes

The KJV wording of Ezekiel 4:9 names six items: wheat, barley, beans,
lentils, millet, and fitches. However, the exact identification of some
ingredients varies between translations:

- **"Beans"** (Hebrew: _pol_): KJV reads "beans"; some modern translations
  read "lentils"
- **"Fitches"** (Hebrew: _kussemeth_): KJV reads "fitches"; some modern
  translations read "spelt" or "vetch"

This recipe follows the KJV interpretation while acknowledging that the
specific ingredients depend on the chosen translation.

## Serving Notes

Serve warm with olive oil or alongside stews. The dense, multi-grain
texture is consistent with the passage's description of emergency bread —
a deliberate contrast to normal, refined bread. The bread is coarser and
denser than modern commercial bread, which is historically appropriate.
