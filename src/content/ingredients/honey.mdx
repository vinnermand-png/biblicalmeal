## Biblical References

Honey appears in eight distinct KJV contexts, each with its own narrative
setting:

**Exodus 3:8** describes the promised land as flowing with milk and honey.
This land-description formula establishes honey as a symbol of abundance, but
the passage does not specify whether the honey is bee honey or another sweet
substance.

**Exodus 16:31** says manna tasted like wafers made with honey. This
comparison establishes honey as a familiar flavor reference, though it does
not resolve the identification question.

**Judges 14:8-9** reports a swarm of bees and honey in the lion carcass,
which Samson took and ate and gave to his parents. This passage explicitly
mentions bees, making it one of the clearest indicators of actual bee honey
in the Bible.

**1 Samuel 14:27** reports Jonathan dipping his rod in an honeycomb and
eating from it. The honeycomb reference is specific and unambiguous.

**2 Samuel 17:29** lists honey among provisions brought for David and the
people with him to eat. This provision context places honey alongside other
food items.

**Proverbs 24:13** calls honey good and pairs it with honeycomb as sweet to
the taste. This wisdom literature context presents honey as a pleasurable
food.

**Matthew 3:4** says John's meat was locusts and wild honey. The phrase "wild
honey" is specific and must not be generalized to all honey references.

**Luke 24:42-43** reports that Jesus was given a piece of broiled fish and
honeycomb, and ate before them. This explicit narrative consumption context
is one of the clearest references to actual honeycomb in the Gospels.

## Historical Context

The KJV uses the English word "honey" to translate the Hebrew term devash,
but scholars have long debated whether this term always refers to bee honey
or sometimes to date syrup or other sweet substances.

The passages that explicitly mention bees (Judges 14:8-9), honeycomb (1 Samuel
14:27, Luke 24:42-43), or wild honey (Matthew 3:4) provide the strongest
evidence for actual bee honey. Other passages — particularly the "land flowing
with milk and honey" formula — remain open to interpretation.

## Archaeological Evidence

The Tel Rehov archaeological site has yielded evidence of ancient beekeeping,
including apiary remains dating to the 10th-9th centuries BCE. This
archaeological evidence supports the existence of organized beekeeping in the
ancient Near East, though the precise relationship between this evidence and
biblical honey references requires careful interpretation.

## What Remains Uncertain

The devash identification question remains active. KJV wording alone does not
resolve bee honey versus syrup in every biblical context. The "milk and
honey" formula remains a land-description and must not be converted into a
precise universal dietary statement.

Wild honey, honeycomb, and the explicit bee narrative remain distinct evidence
categories that should not be generalized to every occurrence of biblical
honey wording.
