## Biblical References

Figs appear in twelve distinct KJV contexts, each with its own narrative
setting:

**Deuteronomy 8:8** names fig trees among the signature agricultural products
of the land — the traditional "seven species" list. This passage establishes
the fig as part of the agricultural identity of the promised land.

**Genesis 3:7** reports the first human garments were sewn from fig leaves.
This narrative context presents the fig tree in the earliest human story,
though the passage concerns covering, not food.

**Numbers 13:23** records the scouts bringing figs back among samples of the
land's produce. This reconnaissance context establishes figs as a recognized
agricultural product of the land.

**1 Samuel 25:18** lists two hundred cakes of figs in Abigail's provision to
David. This passage places fig cakes in a hospitality and provision context.

**1 Samuel 30:12** reports a starving man revived after being given a piece of
fig cake and raisin clusters. This narrative report concerns restoration, not
medical guidance.

**2 Kings 20:7** reports Isaiah ordering a lump of figs laid on Hezekiah's
boil, after which he recovered. This narrative report of an ancient remedy
must never be presented as medical advice.

**Proverbs 27:18** draws its analogy from fig cultivation: whoever keeps the
fig tree eats its fruit. This wisdom context connects fig tending with
reward.

**Habakkuk 3:17** uses a blossoming-less fig tree as total crop-failure
imagery. This prophetic context presents the fig as part of a broader
agricultural picture.

**Amos 7:14** has Amos describing himself as a gatherer of sycomore fruit.
The sycomore is traditionally identified as a distinct species from the
common fig — this identification question remains open.

**Luke 13:6-7** tells of a fig tree planted in a vineyard that bore no fruit
for three years. This parable material presents the fig in symbolic
imagery.

**Matthew 21:19-20** reports the leafy but fruitless fig tree withering at
Jesus' word. This narrative context connects the fig tree with spiritual
lesson.

**Mark 11:13** reports the leafy fig tree had nothing but leaves "for the
time of figs was not yet." The KJV rendering carries a translation nuance
that distinguishes it from other versions.

## Historical Context

The fig's appearance across agricultural, provision, narrative, and symbolic
contexts suggests it was deeply integrated into ancient Israelite life. The
Deuteronomy 8:8 passage groups it with other signature agricultural products,
while the narrative references place it in daily provision and hospitality.

The fig cake references (1 Samuel 25:18, 1 Samuel 30:12) suggest that figs
were processed into a storable form, though the precise preparation methods
remain beyond the current evidence scope.

Historical interpretation remains bounded by the evidence attached to the
verified claims. The draft therefore avoids turning biblical references into
a complete reconstruction of everyday diet or treating later historical
patterns as automatic proof for every biblical period.

## Archaeological Evidence

The Tel Tsaf archaeological study reports charred young common-fig branches
at a Chalcolithic site. The reviewed study allows a qualified interpretation
that the evidence may indicate common-fig cultivation at that site, but the
finding does not establish a universal account of ancient fig cultivation.

This archaeological evidence supports the antiquity of fig presence in the
region while preserving the distinction between site-specific findings and
broad historical claims.

## What Remains Uncertain

The common-fig scope does not resolve every biblical fruit identification. In
particular, the sycomore fruit in Amos 7:14 remains a separate research
question and must not be silently merged into conclusions about the common
fig.

The precise role of figs in the daily diet of ancient Israelites remains
interpretive rather than directly attested from a single source. The narrative
contexts each require careful reading in their own setting.

## Related Foods

Figs connect naturally to other fruits of the ancient Mediterranean table,
including dates, olives, and pomegranates — each with its own evidence and
uncertainty boundaries. The fig also connects to the broader category of
agricultural products named in Deuteronomy 8:8.
